The Life and Research of Agronomist William D. Stegmeier

October 15, 2026 · Nick Stegmeier

For nearly three decades, Kansas State’s agricultural research station at Hays was the center of William D. “Bill” Stegmeier’s working life. In its fields, he bred plants that could give western Kansas farmers more choices. Some became parents of commercial hybrids in India. Others became tools for scientists studying drought and disease. A career rooted in one place reached people farming thousands of miles away.

He was also my grandfather. I began looking into his research with a simple question: what, exactly, had he worked on? The answer led through old field reports, scientific papers, and a memorial written by a colleague who knew him. Together, they describe a scientist whose work traveled far beyond the fields where it began.

Hays, Kansas · 1971–1998

Hays, at the heart of the work

Bill began his forty-year Kansas State career at Garden City in 1958. In 1971, he moved to the Fort Hays Branch Experiment Station, where he worked until his retirement in May 1998. The station, later called the Agricultural Research Center–Hays, was the base for most of his career.1

An experiment station brings agricultural questions down to the scale where they can be tested: a plot of ground, a planting date, a particular variety. At Hays, Bill helped strengthen a program asking what other crops farmers could grow successfully in western Kansas. His sunflower breeding sought better yields and resistance to insects and disease. His work with pearl millet would become the program’s most far-reaching contribution.2

The station and the scientist

The work was shared face to face. A 1984 station brochure describes June and August field days that took visitors into the plots to see crops and judge whether a new practice could help on their own farms. The photograph of Bill explaining pearl millet puts him in that setting: a scientist showing people what he was growing, with the plants right beside him.14

For Bill, the station’s work also reached beyond ordinary working hours. His colleague C. Tom Hash recalled that many Saturdays, Sundays, and vacation days went into making crosses when the plants were ready. Those seasons at Hays connect the research papers to the person behind them: someone willing to give the plants the time they needed.1

From Green Bay to the Great Plains

Bill was born in Green Bay, Wisconsin, on October 4, 1931, to William and Marie Stegmeier. Before his career in crop research, he served in the United States Marine Corps during the Korean War.1

Part of his childhood was spent farther west, in North Dakota. The 1940 census finds him in Elgin, in Grant County, an eight-year-old schoolboy living with his parents and two younger sisters. The enumerator recorded his name as “Wm Duane Stegmeier” and his birthplace as Wisconsin. The same page places him in rural Grant County in 1935. Green Bay was his birthplace; Elgin is now a documented part of the story of where he grew up.12

United States Marine Corps · 1951–1953

Corporal William D. Stegmeier

A North Dakota veterans register records his entry into the Marines in Minneapolis on December 18, 1951, and his separation at Camp Pendleton, California, on December 3, 1953, with the rank of corporal. It lists his residence as Grant County, North Dakota. The entry matches his full name and exact birth date.9

Marine muster rolls add some of the places and units behind those dates. A February 1952 record lists him among recruits at the Marine Corps Recruit Depot in San Diego. Records from 1952 and early 1953 place him in the Signal Company, 3d Signal Battalion, 3d Marine Division, at Camp Pendleton. By October 1953, he appears with the base’s Supporting Arms Training Regiment. These records were matched using the service number in the veterans register.13

His service during the Korean War is confirmed. The records found so far document California assignments; they do not establish a posting in Korea. His full personnel file is the next source to consult for overseas service.

During those years, he and Jean Cromer married in Yuma, Arizona, on January 2, 1953. His education took him to Colorado State University in Fort Collins, where he earned bachelor’s and master’s degrees, and to South Dakota State University in Brookings for further graduate study. A USDA report published in February 1969 records that he had resumed graduate study at South Dakota State while working as an agronomist at Garden City. His learning continued alongside his research career.1, 10

Green Bay, North Dakota, the Marine Corps, and university study were all part of the road that eventually led to Hays. The map brings those places together, with photographs from around the years in his story.

Making room for a different crop

At Hays, the crop that became central to his career was pearl millet, a grain long grown for food and livestock feed in Africa and Asia. It could tolerate heat, low rainfall, and poor sandy soils. Those qualities made it interesting for Kansas, but a plant suited to a tropical growing season would not necessarily produce a useful harvest farther north.

Bill and his colleagues needed plants that flowered early enough, stood short enough for mechanical harvesting, and produced larger seeds. They also needed stalks that stayed upright and grain that ripened evenly. A 1984 bulletin he coauthored describes a program that had screened more than 2,300 lines and varieties in pursuit of those traits.3

Plant breeding makes progress across generations. Breeders choose parents, cross them, grow the offspring, and select plants that combine useful characteristics. Then they repeat the process. The result may be a promising new crop, but it can also be a parent that gives other breeders a better starting point.

The field reports show that progress happening. Early millet trials generally yielded less grain than commercial sorghum. By a 1992 dryland trial at Garden City, reported the following year by Merle Witt and Bill, the experimental millets averaged 26 percent more grain than the sorghum hybrid used for comparison. Stalk strength still needed improvement: too many plants fell over. The report records both the gain and the problem left to solve.3, 5

From Hays to India

Some of Bill’s most influential plants have names that look more like filing codes than agricultural history: 842A and 842B, and 843A and 843B.

These were parental lines used to produce hybrids. An “A” line does not produce functional pollen, so its seed can be produced by crossing it with a chosen pollen parent. The matching “B” line helps breeders maintain the A line. Together, they make it possible to reproduce useful hybrid combinations reliably.

Their history crosses several continents. Bill developed the original stocks at Hays using material that included pearl millet collected from a market in Kumasi, Ghana, in 1949, and breeding lines from Georgia. In 1980, his collaborator D. J. Andrews brought the Kansas stocks to the International Crops Research Institute for the Semi-Arid Tropics, or ICRISAT, in India. Further selection there improved resistance to downy mildew, a serious crop disease.4

The resulting lines became parents of crops grown by farmers. 843A was the seed parent of HHB 67, released in India in 1990; 842A was the seed parent of HHB 68, released in 1993. HHB 67 matured in roughly 65 to 70 days, making it especially useful where the growing season was short.4

Kansas State’s history of the Hays station reported that, by 1997, hybrids descended from his program were grown on more than five million acres in India. The work had begun with a search for alternatives for Kansas farmers. Its reach extended to people farming under very different conditions, thousands of miles away.2

Beyond pearl millet

Bill also worked to make sunflower a more useful crop for Kansas, breeding for better yields and resistance to insects and disease. He participated in sunflower performance testing and in the regional effort to develop canola suited to the Great Plains. The canola reports list him among a large group of researchers working across states and institutions.2, 6

His collaborations included plant disease research. In a 1996 paper, Bill, D. L. Seifers, T. L. Harvey, and K. D. Kofoid documented natural infection of pearl millet and sorghum by wheat streak mosaic virus in Kansas. Tests in the laboratory and greenhouse, including experiments with the tiny mites that transmit the virus, helped establish that these crops could be infected.7

That kind of finding matters to a breeder. A crop’s value depends on how it behaves in the field: whether it emerges, survives disease, stays standing, and produces grain when farmers need it.

A breeder’s work keeps growing

Bill retired in May 1998 and died that July, aged 66. The plants he had developed continued to appear in other people’s experiments.

A 2002 research report describes scientists using 843B as a foundation for breeding and genetic studies, and improving the parents of HHB 67 for disease resistance and yield. Another report from that year documents 843A and 843B in work on drought tolerance.8

This is one way to understand his contribution. A successful breeding line can become both a crop parent and a research tool. Other scientists can use it to ask new questions, make new crosses, and develop plants for conditions its original breeder never had the chance to study.

In his memorial, ICRISAT breeder C. Tom Hash emphasized how freely Bill shared his research materials, often before publication. The plants themselves carried much of his contribution to the people who could use it.1

Bill and “Pearl”

The memorial also preserves a few details that belong beside the scientific record.

His dedication to the Hays breeding program followed him home. Bill planted isolated millet plots in his own garden to keep unwanted pollen out of his crosses. Jean joked about the other woman in his life. When people asked what Bill was doing, she would say he was “still messing around with Pearl.”

His children remembered him coming home from trips talking about the scientists he had met, impressed by their knowledge and grateful for their friendship. They remembered his concern for feeding people efficiently and economically. Away from the research plots, he served his community through the local improvement district, rural fire district, and zoning board.1

For me, those recollections give the papers a person to belong to. There is the breeder whose plants became part of crops grown across the world, and there is Bill, working in the garden, admiring his colleagues, and taking the time to show someone a head of millet. I want to preserve that picture of him, too.
